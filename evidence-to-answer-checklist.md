# TSIA2 Reading Practice: An Evidence-to-Answer Checklist

A reading question can feel difficult even when the passage is understandable. The usual problem is not vocabulary. It is the gap between noticing an idea and proving that one answer choice matches the passage better than the others.

This checklist turns that gap into a short, repeatable routine. It is designed for independent TSIA2-style reading practice, not as an official testing rule.

## 1. Name the task before reading the choices

Rewrite the question stem in a few words:

- **main point** — What is the passage mostly trying to establish?
- **detail** — Which statement does the passage directly support?
- **inference** — What must be true based on the evidence?
- **purpose** — Why did the author include this sentence or example?
- **meaning in context** — What does the word or phrase do here?

This prevents an attractive answer from changing the task in your mind.

## 2. Mark the evidence boundary

Find the smallest useful evidence window. For a detail question, this may be one sentence plus the sentence before it. For a main-point question, it may be the opening, the final claim, and repeated ideas.

Do not treat the whole passage as equally important. A defined evidence boundary reduces rereading and makes comparison easier.

## 3. Predict the answer in plain language

Before evaluating the choices, write or say a rough prediction. It does not need to be elegant. A prediction such as “the author thinks the change helped, but only under limited conditions” is enough.

The purpose is to create a standard against which the options can be tested.

## 4. Test every important word

An answer can be mostly correct and still fail because of one word. Pay special attention to:

- absolute terms such as *always*, *never*, and *entirely*;
- scope words such as *some*, *many*, and *most*;
- cause-and-effect verbs;
- comparisons;
- time references;
- claims about the author’s attitude.

Ask: “Can I point to evidence for this exact wording?”

## 5. Label the distractor, not just the answer

When an option is wrong, give it a short label:

- **outside scope** — related topic, unsupported claim;
- **too broad** — expands beyond the passage;
- **too narrow** — captures a detail but misses the task;
- **reversed** — switches cause, contrast, or viewpoint;
- **half true** — begins accurately, then adds an unsupported idea;
- **right evidence, wrong question** — uses a real detail for a different task.

This makes review more useful than simply recording the correct letter.

## 6. Keep a two-line error log

After a missed question, record:

1. the evidence that should have controlled the decision;
2. the distractor pattern that pulled you away from it.

For example:

> Evidence: the author limits the claim to the pilot program.  
> Error: I chose an option that generalized the result to all programs.

Over several practice sessions, repeated labels reveal the process that needs attention.

## A compact practice card

Use this six-line card beside a practice set:

1. What is the task?
2. Where is the evidence boundary?
3. What is my plain-language prediction?
4. Which exact words must be supported?
5. What distractor pattern appears?
6. What belongs in the error log?
